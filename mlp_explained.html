2. What Does "Bending" Look Like? (GELU vs ReLU)

The activation function is the part that lets the MLP make decisions. Without it, the whole thing collapses into boring multiplication. Here's what the two most common bending functions actually do:

ReLU is like a strict bouncer: "Negative number? OUT (zero). Positive? Come on in." Sharp corner at zero.

GELU is the same idea but smoother — it lets a tiny bit of slightly-negative stuff through, like a friendlier bouncer. The smoothness helps training go better, which is why modern transformers (GPT, BERT) prefer it.

Why does bending matter? If you only stack matrix multiplications (no bending), you can prove mathematically that 100 layers do the same thing as 1 layer. The bend is what gives the network the power to learn anything interesting. It's the "decision-making" step.

3. Why Expand to 4× Bigger?

Think of it like this: imagine you have a compressed zip file of all your school notes — small, tight, dense. To actually read and edit your notes, you have to unzip them first. That's what the expansion does.

The locker analogy. Your word vector (512 numbers) is like a tightly packed school locker. Everything's crammed in. The MLP says: "Let me dump it all out on a giant table (2048 spots)" — now there's space to sort, label, and decide what matters. Then it picks the important stuff and puts it back in the locker (512 again).

Why specifically 4×? Honestly, it's mostly experimental — researchers tried different sizes and 4× turned out to be a sweet spot between:

Smaller (e.g., 2×)4× (the standard)Larger (e.g., 8×)
Fast & cheap, but the model can't store as many patternsEnough room to store lots of facts, training is stable, GPU memory manageableMore capacity, but training gets slower and harder, often not worth it

4. The MLP as a "Memory Bank"

Here's the most interesting research finding from the last few years: those big weight matrices inside the MLP behave like a key-value lookup table.

The expand step (W₁) acts like a giant set of questions: "Is this a capital city? Is this a fruit? Is this a verb?" Each of the 2048 neurons is asking one such question. The bending step (GELU) keeps only the questions that got a strong "yes." The shrink step (W₂) then mixes the answers back into a normal-sized vector full of relevant knowledge.

5. Putting It All Together

Final analogy: A transformer block is like one round of class.
  • Attention = students discussing — sharing what they each understand.
  • MLP = each student opening their own textbook to look things up and think privately.
Stack many rounds (12, 96, sometimes more) and the model gets really smart.

7. Residual Connections — the Shortcut Arrows

Imagine playing telephone with 50 people in a line — the message gets garbled. But what if each person also kept a sticky note of the original message and added it back at every step? The message would survive. That's a residual connection.

output = x + Block(x) — the original input is added back after the block.

Why this matters: Without residuals, you can't train deep networks at all — the signal gets lost. With them, you can stack 96+ layers and training works fine. This single idea (from a 2015 paper called ResNet) made modern deep learning possible.

8. Layer Normalization — the Leveling Step

As numbers flow through layers, they tend to either explode or shrink to zero. Layer Norm fixes this by putting each vector on a "standard scale."

Grading analogy: School A grades out of 10, School B grades out of 100. To compare students fairly, you'd convert everyone's scores to percentiles. Layer Norm does the same thing — for each word vector, it centers the numbers around 0 and rescales them to a normal range, so the next layer always sees something predictable.

The math is simple: subtract the average, divide by the spread. Then two tiny learnable knobs (γ and β) let the model rescale if it wants. That's it.

9. The Full Transformer Block

Now let's put everything together. This is what one block actually looks like (using the modern "Pre-Norm" style — what GPT and LLaMA use):

Read it top to bottom:

  1. Input x comes in.
  2. Normalize it (LayerNorm).
  3. Run Attention — words share info.
  4. Add original x back (first residual).
  5. Normalize again.
  6. Run the MLP — each word thinks privately.
  7. Add the post-attention vector back (second residual).
  8. Pass to the next block.
Stacking blocks: Repeat this exact block 12 times for a small model, 96 times for GPT-3, or even more for the biggest models today. Each block is identical in shape — just with its own learned weights.

10. The Whole Picture

A transformer is a tower of identical blocks. In each block: words first chat with each other (Attention), then each word goes home to study privately (MLP). Layer Norm keeps the numbers tidy. Residual connections keep the original message alive all the way through. Stack 'em high, train on enormous text, and you've got something that can write essays, code, and poetry.

That's it — you now know the full anatomy of a transformer block.
